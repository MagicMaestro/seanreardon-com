I needed analytics that would respect the audience, stay cheap, and not make me write a privacy policy that read like an apology. Three separate candidates failed those requirements in three different ways — and one of them failed against a rule I was writing that same afternoon.

The rule: *self-hosted and privacy-respecting comes first; paid services get flagged explicitly — and "free with my data" is its own kind of paid.* The candidate: Google Analytics. The rule would have caught it. I just hadn't applied it yet. A few hours later I noticed, and switched again.

This site is a personal portfolio — projects, writing, and retrospectives like this one. It's read mostly by tech leads, engineers, and other technical professionals — people who notice when their own browser tab fills up with third-party trackers, and who run blockers because they'd become tired of it. Whatever I picked for analytics needed to fit that posture. It also needed to cost less, in time and money, than the value of the data it produced — which for a site like this is "do people read the writing, and which posts." Not a lot.

I started with Plausible CE, the self-hosted version. Privacy-first, no cookies, no consent banner needed. "I run my own infra" is a credible signal for the audience I was aiming at, and Plausible CE was the obvious way to send it. The install went through cleanly: a Docker stack on a stats subdomain, with the database and analytics store running alongside the app, plus a security-monitoring service that needed to be told about the new files. Smoke-tested. Working.

Then I looked at what I'd added: three new services on a VPS, each with their own patching cadence, on a personal site. The operational cost was wrong for the value. Analytics for a portfolio doesn't justify three more things to maintain. I tore it down the same day.

1st Failure: operational cost — realizing the development effort was out of proportion to the need.

So I moved to Google Analytics 4 (GA4). Free, ubiquitous, zero infra. I started writing the redesign tasks it would need — a cookie consent banner, IP anonymization, 14-month data retention, Consent Mode v2, a privacy-policy section disclosing the Google integration.

That list is where the catch came. The site's posture is audience-respect; that was already the design principle, already in the standing rules. GA4's compliance work isn't optional cosmetic effort — it's the cost of using a tool whose business model is data. The consent banner GA4 needs is a friction my audience would notice and block, accurately reading it as a tracker. The privacy policy disclosure ("we share data with Google") reads as friction even to readers who never check the network tab.

And sitting on the desk next to all of this — literally, in the same day's notes — I was writing the standing rule for the project: *paid services flagged explicitly; "free with my data" is its own kind of paid.* GA4 is the canonical "free with my data" service. The rule was being written. Yet I was picking the very tool it would have flagged.

2nd Failure: rule mismatch — delayed reaction to a rule I'd just written. Should have caught the mismatch at hour one. Caught at hour three instead.

The second pivot didn't happen because I sat down and re-evaluated. It happened because I was reviewing the image-hosting decision a few hours later, and Cloudflare came up. *Cloudflare. They also do analytics.* That's it. A passing thought, in the middle of a different conversation. The candidate that ended up being right surfaced not by analysis, but by being mentioned adjacent to something else.

Cloudflare Web Analytics is cookieless. Aggregate-only. No consent banner needed, because there's nothing to consent to. Free at this site's scale. No data trade. It satisfied every requirement, including the rule I was writing.

And it had been available the whole time. I'd just never put it on the candidate list.

3rd & Final Failure: failure to consider all tools in the first place. This was not a failure of the tool. It was mine — the right analytics tool was invisible until I'd ruled out two wrong ones, because I hadn't gone looking for it.

The whole sequence pairs with another decision from the same week, which I wrote about in [the privacy-compliance post](/lessons-learned/compliant-with-laws-that-dont-apply): why this site complies with GDPR and CPRA-lite when it legally meets neither threshold. That post is about going beyond the legal floor as a signal. This one is about catching yourself short of the floor you've already set for yourself. Both are versions of the same question — what does the audience deserve, and is the current plan delivering it? It's a question you have to keep asking. Including, sometimes, in the middle of executing the answer.

The lesson isn't "pick Cloudflare Web Analytics." It's that the criteria do the work — the candidates only matter once you've actually held them up to the rules you said you cared about. I had the criteria. I just hadn't run them yet.

Two pivots in a day looks indecisive. It wasn't. The requirements held steady; I just kept finding candidates that hadn't met them. The third one did. The whole arc cost me an afternoon and a Docker stack — a much cheaper price than shipping the wrong default.
