There's a fear running through pretty much every industry right now — quiet, persistent, mostly unspoken at meetings — that AI is going to do the technical parts of our jobs better than we can. Engineers fear it. Designers fear it. Writers, lawyers, accountants, illustrators, doctors. Pick a field; someone is having the conversation. And the fear isn't unreasonable. The technology is moving fast, and the headlines are loud... and sometimes, scary.

Until pretty recently, I was a holdout. Not loudly — I'm not the kind of person who rants at conferences — but quietly, in the way you put off opening a document you're afraid to read. I used the surface-level stuff: image generators for mood boards, ChatGPT for one-off prompts, tab-completing the obvious. Anything more capable than that — agents, multi-step tools, anything that felt like delegation — I avoided. I told myself I was being careful. The honest truth was that I didn't want to find out that the technical aspects of the job — which I'd relied upon so heavily throughout my career — were now better managed by someone, or rather something, else. To put it bluntly, I was afraid of becoming obsolete.

That's not a great place to operate from. Fear-driven avoidance doesn't make you better at your job; it just makes the world quieter for a while. But eventually, regardless of how you've chosen to approach it, the changing world always catches up.

What changed wasn't a single moment. Rather, it was a series of moments. Instances where my curiosity outweighed those fears. Times when I simply couldn't contain myself and felt the need to further explore this ever-evolving technology. It was watching what happened around me when I'd stop avoiding and start steering — when I asked a model to draft something I'd planned to write from scratch, then read what came back with the same care I'd give a junior developer's pull request. Sometimes the output was almost there. Sometimes it was wrong in interesting ways. Once in a while it genuinely surprised me. None of those outcomes mean *AI replaces me*. All of them mean *I just got back four hours of my Saturday*.

The redesign of this site has been the proving ground. A few examples of what "integration" actually meant in practice:

- **Cataloging an old code-gated app.** I wanted to preserve a Disney trip planner I'd built years ago for family vacations — overengineered the way all good personal projects are. Cataloging it by hand would have been a Saturday I didn't have. I handed it to an agent that walked the directory, read every PHP file, and produced a thirty-plus entry catalog: file purpose, the access-code logic, sanitized config notes, design elements worth lifting into a future Angular rewrite. Two hours instead of a weekend — and frankly, the catalog is more thorough than what I'd have produced doing it by hand.

- **Diagnosing a cross-stack 404.** The newsletter signup form on this site posts straight to a self-hosted Listmonk install. During integration, Listmonk started returning a branded 404 page on every settings save. The bug turned out to be ModSecurity blocking PUT requests three layers up the stack, before the request ever reached Listmonk — the response just *looked* like a Listmonk error because Listmonk was the last system to touch the bytes. A diagnostic agent traced it across Apache, the OWASP Core Rule Set, cPanel's error-document routing, and the reverse proxy in about an afternoon. I'd have wasted a weekend chasing the wrong layer.

- **Splitting infrastructure work to its own agent.** The server-side work for this rebuild — root systemd, Postgres, Apache configs — has its own dedicated agent, separate from the one assisting with the site itself. Rather than context-switching between site work and infrastructure work, I write a brief: what I need, why, smoke tests, hand-back format. The infrastructure agent executes it. There's a folder of seven of these briefs by now. The interface looks weirdly like a normal small team.

The pattern across all of those: I write better prompts than I did a year ago. I set up purpose-driven agents instead of one-shot prompting. And I review every output in detail before shipping, because the model is good but it isn't perfect, and the bar is the same one I'd hold a teammate to. AI tooling shortens the distance between *I see the bug* and *I see the fix*, but it still requires the developer to steer the work and check it carefully. Six years of writing C#/.NET and Angular gave me the judgment to tell the model when it's right and when it's hallucinating. That judgment is what makes the integration valuable. Without it, you're just generating slop faster.

The thing I had wrong was who AI would advantage most. I'd assumed it would displace the people who'd spent careers on a single specialty. And to some degree, I still believe that's true. This is an exciting-yet-scary new technology that, within a short period of time, will be able to exceed even some of the leading experts in terms of technical output. However, those using that knowledge and experience while embracing AI will be able to steer this ship in new and exciting ways. But what's perhaps even cooler to think about is what AI does for the non-experts: for the curious-but-not-specialist explorers — the everyday tinkerers and builders who've always had more ideas than time — AI raises the floor. Shipping those ideas without heavy investment or expertise has become a reality. And if that's true, then just imagine what can be produced when we put this advanced tool into the hands of an experienced professional.

All of this means that the kind of person who quietly maintains four side projects, learns adjacent stacks for fun, and ships small improvements on hobby tools — that person now has leverage they didn't have a year ago. Those people aren't new in the world. But what's new is that the typical roadblocks of a one-person team have now been lifted, and their more ambitious ideas can be made into a reality.

The ceiling on what one designer can ship has moved. The constraint isn't time anymore — it's curiosity. Project ideas I've kept on the backlog for months (or even years) because I considered them "too much work for a team of one" are coming back to the table and rolling out as v1s in days, not months. A bunch of these idea-only projects — ones I'd started to believe would never see the light of day — are now things I'm anticipating shipping this year! I don't know exactly where AI will lead us as a society. But I, for one, am excited to find out what comes next.
